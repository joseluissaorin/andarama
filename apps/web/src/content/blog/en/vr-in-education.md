**VR in education** doesn't start with headsets. It starts with immersive content (a 360 tour of a real place, with explanations and questions pinned where they belong) that runs on what your classroom already has: the interactive display, Chromebooks or iPads, and students' phones. Cardboard viewers add immersion for a few dollars each; standalone headsets take it further, but they are an optional layer you can add next school year. This article covers what works in 2026 at each level of equipment, when headsets are worth the money and which mistakes keep repeating.

## The right order: content, then screens, then headsets

Most classroom VR projects that fail do so for the same reason: the headsets were bought first and the question of what to do with them came later. They end up in a closet, because charging, updating, handing out and cleaning twenty headsets for a twenty-minute activity isn't sustainable, and because the content in the app stores rarely lines up with the curriculum or your state standards.

The order that works is the opposite:

1. **Your own or adapted content** that fits the unit: a virtual field trip, an escape room, a 360 lab walkthrough.
2. **The screens you already have**: the classroom display, Chromebooks, tablets, phones. This is where ninety percent of the work happens.
3. **Cardboard viewers** for cheap immersion and, if the project takes hold, **a few standalone headsets** on rotation.

With a web-based tour, all three levels use **the same link**. You don't have to produce three versions.

## Level 0: the classroom display and Chromebooks

A 360 tour on the front-of-room display already changes the lesson: the teacher turns the view, zooms in on a detail, opens the pinned explanation and puts the question to the class. On Chromebooks or tablets, each group explores at its own pace, using the gyroscope if the device has one or dragging with a finger.

## Level 1: cardboard viewers and a phone

A cardboard viewer (or a plastic phone headset) costs very little and turns any phone into a stereoscopic viewer. Andarama's **cardboard mode** splits the screen in two, uses the gyroscope to follow the student's head and triggers hotspots by gaze: a dot in the center and a ring that fills while you hold your gaze on a marker. The physical button on the viewer also works as a tap.

It's the cheapest immersion there is, and for a ten-minute virtual field trip per student it's more than enough. A few things to know:

- Phones go in **landscape**; the viewer warns you if the phone is upright.
- On iPhone, students have to accept the motion and orientation permission the first time.
- Which hotspots can be used inside the viewer (all of them, navigation only, or a selection) is decided **per tour**, in Settings, and the gaze dwell time is adjustable (2.5 seconds by default).

The details are in the [VR guide](https://docs.andarama.com/en/usuario/realidad-virtual/).

## Level 2: standalone headsets (when they're worth it)

A Meta Quest, a Pico or similar gives you what cardboard can't: hand tracking, controllers, floating panels that are easy to read and a real sense of presence. With Andarama there's nothing to install: open the tour address in the headset's browser and tap **VR Mode**. Hands appear with their joints, a ray points, and a thumb-and-index pinch selects; quizzes, galleries, videos and the comparison slider are drawn inside the session, so a tour with questions can be finished without taking the headset off.

They're worth it when:

- There's a stable **VR station** in the school (the library, a makerspace, the tech lab) with four or five charged headsets and one person who looks after them.
- The content calls for it: a safety walkthrough on a factory floor, a space you need to "feel" at scale (a cathedral, a cave), a career and technical education simulation.
- They're used on **rotation** by groups, in ten-minute sessions, while everyone else works on Chromebooks.

They're not worth it when they're bought for the whole class at once, when nobody is there to maintain them, or when the plan is "watch 360 videos on YouTube".

A note on age: headset makers set age requirements for their devices and accounts (often with parent-managed accounts for younger kids), so check the manufacturer's policy and your district's rules before planning headset use with younger students. Cardboard and screens don't have that problem.

## What to avoid

- **Free movement.** That's what makes people sick. 360 tours teleport between scenes; keep sessions short.
- **Apps you have to install on every headset.** Every update costs an afternoon. A web tour is updated on the server and everyone sees the new version.
- **Content locked into one platform.** If the vendor changes next year, the work is lost. Andarama tours export as a ZIP, a single HTML file, a PWA or a SCORM package, and the software is open source: the material keeps working even if everything else changes.
- **Making VR the goal.** It's a medium. If the question doesn't require looking around, it doesn't need 360.

## A realistic plan for a school

| Semester | What to do | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Fall | One virtual field trip and one escape room on the classroom display and Chromebooks, with one teacher | Classroom plan ($42 a year, five tours) or a free self-hosted install; a borrowed or mid-range 360 camera |
| Spring | Cardboard viewers for one class; students take their own 360 photos on a real field trip | A few dollars per viewer |
| Next year | Four standalone headsets in the library, on rotation, for the tours that already exist | The headset budget and one person in charge |

In that order, the content exists before the hardware, and the hardware is bought knowing what it's for.
